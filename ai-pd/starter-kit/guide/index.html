From chatbot to co-worker: a teacher's guide to building with Claude

Noel Robert · 3 November 2026

I spent a month making coursebooks, exam papers and learning apps with AI. I made almost every mistake you can make. This guide is so you don't have to.

How to read this guide

Every section has two tracks.

The main text is for every teacher. Short steps, plain words, no jargon. If you only read this, you'll be fine.

"Under the hood" boxes are for the IT-minded: the IT teacher, the timetabler who writes spreadsheet formulas, the colleague who sets up the smartboards. They explain the same step in technical terms, with the extra detail you'd want before trusting it.

Note: too busy, or does all this reading make you anxious? Hand it to Claude.
You don't have to read this guide or do any of the steps yourself. Open Claude, paste in this guide's link (and my Teacher Starter Kit folder: drive.google.com/drive/folders/1yd9LqF8LvNslLLT4r5jjhqtyRyHYUuxK), and say: "Read this guide and take me through it step by step. Do every technical step yourself and only stop me for sign-ins or approvals." Claude does the setting up; you just click Allow now and then, and get back to your learners.

The single most important idea: you are the teacher, Claude is the technician. You decide what good looks like. Claude does the installing, uploading, formatting, checking and fixing. If you find yourself doing technical chores, stop and ask Claude to do them.

Under the hood · for IT staffClaude here means Anthropic's assistant in three forms: the claude.ai chat, claude.ai Projects (persistent workspaces with their own instructions and files), and Claude Code, an agentic coding tool that can run shell commands, edit files, drive a browser and work across sessions. The expert's lens, and the plumbing

A great textbook doesn't come from a clever prompt. It comes from an expert's lens, and from plumbing that makes sure Claude looks through that lens every single time.

The lens is you. Claude has read nearly everything, so it can produce something generic and pretty-looking in seconds. That's what most people get from a quick chat. What it doesn't have is your judgement: what matters for these learners, this curriculum, this exam board, your classroom. A lens is that judgement, written down. The teacher is the expert; that part can't be handed over.

You'll need several lenses. Mine took weeks of background research before the first good page appeared:

  • Curriculum lens: exact SAGS wording, exam weighting, what examiners reward.
  • Learning-science lens: retrieval practice, worked examples that fade, spacing, feedback (Rosenshine and others).
  • Learner lens: weaker readers who need it clear and calm; bright learners who need a stretch.
  • Print and design lens: answer space sized from the real worked answer, page spreads, greyscale-safe colour.
  • School lens: house style, crest, moderation labels, versions.

The plumbing makes the lenses stick. In a normal chat, Claude forgets your lens the moment the chat ends or gets too long. Plumbing means putting every lens in files that Claude reads automatically before every job: a rules file, a design-lens skill, a hub folder, a backup. That's what the "Start here" steps build. It's boring to set up, so ask Claude to set it up for you.

So when a colleague asks "what prompt did you use?", the honest answer is: no single prompt. It's the lens folder plus the plumbing. That's why I share one folder, my Teacher Starter Kit, instead of a prompt.

Under the hood · for IT staffStages 1 to 3 kept all state in conversation history or the cloud; stage 4 moved execution to the local machine (Claude Code via Remote Control, with native file access to the Drive-synced folder); stage 5 moved state to disk and version control (CLAUDE.md, skills, Git, a private remote, scheduled Takeout). The pattern generalises: anything you want to survive the session belongs in a file, not a chat.
Under the hood · for IT staffA lens is durable, curated context. The model's general knowledge is broad but unweighted; the lens supplies the weighting (priorities, constraints, acceptance criteria) for a specific domain. The plumbing is how that context is injected deterministically rather than by memory: a CLAUDE.md the harness loads at session start, a skill whose description triggers on matching tasks, reference files loaded on demand, and version control so the lens itself evolves with a history. Without plumbing, quality depends on what happened to be in the conversation; with it, every session starts from the same spec.

Start here: the foundation I wish I had on day one

Do these five things once, in your first session. Ask Claude to do each one for you; you only need to sign in when it asks.

  1. Install the Claude desktop app on the computer where your school files are (or are synced, e.g. Google Drive for desktop), and turn on the Claude in Chrome extension so Claude can work in your browser. Ask Claude: "Check I have everything installed and switched on."
  2. Make a hub folder in your Drive, e.g. 00 Teaching Hub, holding your rules (CLAUDE.md), an index of your projects and links, and your house style. Every future project starts by reading it. Ask Claude: "Set up my hub folder and fill it from what you know about my work."
  3. Write down your design lens: what makes a good page, worksheet or app for your learners. Mine is a learning-science checklist (retrieval, worked examples, spacing, feedback). Ask Claude: "Turn my design lens into a skill you apply automatically" (or start from my Teacher Starter Kit).
  4. Create a personal GitHub account with your personal email, not your school one. It gives you version history, a private backup and free hosting of learner pages. You won't need to understand Git. Ask Claude: "Set up GitHub for me. Only stop me to sign in."
  5. Set up a backup that copies your hub, sources and finished work to that private account. Ask Claude: "Set up a one-click backup of my teaching work." After that, just say "refresh my backup".

That's it. From then on every project begins with "read my hub", and you never re-explain yourself.

Under the hood · for IT staffCompaction is the context-window manager summarising older turns to free tokens; anything not written to disk or a memory file is lossy after that. Usage limits are token-based, and a long thread re-sends its whole context on every turn, so cost grows faster than linearly with thread length. The cure is state on disk: a CLAUDE.md the harness loads at session start, project memory files, and Git for artefacts.

Let Claude do the technical work

This is the most important section. You should not be the technician. My biggest frustration was being asked "click here, now click there" dozens of times. Most of that was unnecessary: Claude can do it.

What Claude can do for you, if you let it:

  • Install software, create folders, copy and back up files on your computer.
  • Work in your Chrome browser: open sites, fill in forms, create things on GitHub, set up Google Takeout.
  • Look at your screen to see what's wrong, instead of you sending screenshots.
  • Publish learner pages, upload backups, keep version history.
  • Check its own work: render the PDF, open the app at phone size, cross-check a memo against the paper.

How to switch on autopilot:

  1. Say it plainly at the start: "Do the technical steps yourself. Only stop me for sign-ins, passwords or approvals." (Better still, put that line in your rules file once.)
  2. Turn on the tools it needs: the Claude in Chrome extension, and Computer use in the desktop app's settings (lets Claude click in desktop apps such as GitHub Desktop).
  3. When starting a build, say "use remote control on my computer" so it works on your machine, not in the cloud.
  4. Step away. If you're using the same browser at the same time, you'll get in each other's way.

What still needs you (by design, for your safety): signing in, passwords and passkeys, approving access ("Authorize" buttons), and anything that spends money or sends messages for you. Claude will tell you exactly which button, one at a time, then check your screen itself.

Under the hood · for IT staffClaude Code loads ~/.claude/CLAUDE.md (global) and any CLAUDE.md in the working directory at session start; I keep the global file as a short pointer to the hub so one Drive file governs every project, and Codex reads ~/.codex/AGENTS.md pointing to the same place. Skills live in ~/.claude/skills/<name>/SKILL.md with a frontmatter description that decides when they trigger; put long reference material in references/ so it loads only when needed. The backup is a robocopy-plus-git script pushing to a private repo, with Git Credential Manager handling the OAuth token. Set core.autocrlf false and a * -text .gitattributes so PDFs aren't corrupted by line-ending conversion, and exclude desktop.ini and .gdoc stubs (Google Docs on Drive for desktop are pointer files; export them with Google Takeout).
Under the hood · for IT staffClaude Code runs shell commands (PowerShell or Bash) under a permission mode; auto mode lets a classifier approve routine actions so you aren't prompted for each one, while destructive or outward-facing actions still need confirmation. Browser control goes through the Claude in Chrome extension (DOM-aware: it reads the accessibility tree, clicks by element reference and runs page JavaScript), so it's faster and more precise than pixel-clicking. Desktop control ("computer use") is off by default and is granted per app; browsers are read-only through it, and terminals click-only. Credentials are never typed by the model: OAuth flows (GitHub Desktop, Git Credential Manager) hand the token to the OS credential store after you click Authorize. One gotcha on locked-down school laptops: PowerShell script execution may be disabled, so run scripts with -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -File per process rather than changing machine policy.

Your toolbox: skills, connectors, plugins and extensions

Out of the box, Claude only sees what you type. Four kinds of add-on change that. You switch each one on yourself, and you can switch it off again.

  • Connectors let Claude reach your other apps, such as Google Drive, Gmail, Calendar or Canva, so it can find and file things for you. You turn them on in Claude's settings and approve what each one may do. Connect only what you need.
  • Skills are saved know-how: a set of instructions Claude loads automatically when they're relevant. Built-in skills let it make proper Word, PowerPoint, Excel and PDF files. You can add your own. My "design lens" is a skill: it makes every resource follow the same learning-science rules. Ask Claude to turn your own house rules into a skill, and it will tell you where to add it.
  • Plugins are ready-made bundles of skills and connectors for a kind of work, like design or productivity. Switch on only the ones you actually use. I switched off the finance, marketing and similar ones, because they just add noise.
  • Extensions let Claude work beyond the chat window. Claude in Chrome can read and click through websites in your browser, like Canvas or the IEB document library, with your permission. The Claude desktop app can work with folders on your own computer. Claude Code builds websites and apps: my learning studios and this presentation were made with it.

Not sure which to use? Ask Claude: "What should I switch on for what I want to do, and is it safe?" And one rule for all four: connect only what you need, and keep learner data out.

Under the hood · for IT staffConnectors are MCP (Model Context Protocol) servers: each exposes named tools with their own OAuth scopes, granted per user and revocable in settings. Skills are folders with a SKILL.md whose description decides when the model loads them; only the description sits in context until then. Plugins package skills, connectors, commands and hooks so they can be enabled or disabled as a set, which also keeps the tool list short. Claude in Chrome acts in the user's own signed-in browser and asks before site access and sensitive actions; Claude Code and the desktop app work on local files under a permission mode.

Which model, which effort level, and when to use Ultracode

Three settings sit at the bottom of the Claude window. Think of them like this:

  • Model = which brain. Bigger models are smarter but use your allowance faster. Lighter ones (Haiku, Sonnet) are quick and cheap; the top ones (Opus, and Fable where your plan offers it) are for the hardest work.
  • Effort (low, medium, high, max) = how long it thinks before answering. More thinking means fewer mistakes on hard jobs, but it's slower and costs more.
  • Ultracode = lets Claude split a huge job across a team of helper agents working in parallel. Powerful, but it uses limits very fast. Off unless the job is genuinely huge.

The easy way: let Claude decide. I've told Claude, in my rules file, to start every new job by recommending the model, effort and Ultracode setting, and saying whether to change them. You can do the same: add "At the start of every new task, tell me the best model, effort level and whether Ultracode should be on" to your rules.

Under the hood · for IT staffEffort controls the model's reasoning budget per turn (extended thinking tokens); higher effort improves multi-step correctness but raises latency and token spend. Ultracode authorises the Workflow tool, which orchestrates many subagents deterministically: excellent for embarrassingly parallel work (per-module review, per-file verification), wasteful for linear writing. Model choice trades capability against rate-limit consumption; the 1M-context variants help when a session must hold a whole book's sources at once. Settings apply from the next turn, so change them before you send the request. The model can't silently change its own model or effort; it can recommend, and switch only when you explicitly ask.

Where to work: chat, cloud projects, or your own computer

My rule of thumb: plan in the cloud, build on your computer.

WhereBest forNot for
claude.ai chatQuestions, research, one email or letter, thinking something throughAnything that makes many files or runs over several days
A project thread in the cloud (the default)Planning, reading SAGS and examiner reports, research. Runs even when your computer is offBuilding books or apps: it can't see your folders and can't upload big files
A project thread on your computer ("use remote control on my computer")Building textbooks, exam papers, memos and apps; anything that must land in your Drive and be print-checkedTimes when your computer is off or asleep

Organise projects by subject or product (Textbooks, Grade 11–12 Physical Sciences, Examinations), and inside each one use one thread per job ("Gr 11 Paper 1", "Module 3: Electrostatics"). Short, focused threads stay cheaper and never lose their rules, because the rules live in your hub.

Under the hood · for IT staffA cloud thread runs in an ephemeral container with a fresh clone of any linked repo; it reaches Drive only through the Google Drive connector, which has a roughly 10 MB upload ceiling and treats Google Docs as API objects. A Remote Control session runs Claude Code on your own machine with your PATH, credentials and synced Drive (G:\My Drive on Windows), so file I/O is native and unlimited, local skills and CLAUDE.md load, and headless Chromium/Playwright can render and inspect the real PDFs. The trade-off is availability: the host must be awake, so keep long builds plugged in with sleep disabled.

ChatGPT or Claude: which for what, and why

Both are excellent; they just suit different jobs. This is what I found in a busy teaching term, not a lab test.

Making a picture, poster image or cartoonChatGPTIt creates images directly. Claude makes diagrams as drawings or code instead.

Building a whole coursebook, memo or exam paper to a strict specClaude, in Claude CodeIt follows long rule files consistently, works through many files, and checks its own work

The honest trade-off: Claude's careful, detail-heavy way of working uses more of your allowance on big jobs. For quick everyday questions, a lighter setting (or either tool) is fine. For the coursebook that represents a year of your teaching, the extra care is worth it.

Why I switched for my coursebook: ChatGPT kept giving me something that looked fine at a glance but didn't hold the detail: exact SAGS wording, answer spaces that fit the real working, page breaks that print properly. Claude, given my lens and rules, kept to them across hundreds of pages.

Under the hood · for IT staffThe difference shows most in long-context, multi-document, rule-bound work. Claude's large context windows (up to 1M tokens in some variants) let it hold a syllabus, a spec and many past papers at once, and Claude Code adds an agentic loop: it reads files, writes code, runs it, inspects the output and iterates, with tools for the shell, browser and file system. ChatGPT is strong at fast conversational turns and has native image generation and a broad plug-in and app ecosystem. Cost scales with tokens processed: reading many large files and reasoning at high effort is precisely what consumes allowance, so match the model and effort to the job. Both vendors change their products quickly; re-test before you standardise a department on either.

Your first textbook, app or learning space: the recipe

The same six steps work for a coursebook, an exam paper or an app.

  1. Gather your sources in one Drive folder: the curriculum document (SAGS, CAPS or syllabus), past papers, examiner reports, your notes, any old textbook chapters.
  2. Start a thread on your computer and ask for a plan first: "Use remote control on my computer. Read my hub. I want to build [the thing] for [grade and subject] from the sources in [folder]. Recommend the model and effort, then show me a plan before building anything."
  3. Approve the plan, or change it. This is where your expertise matters most: sequence, emphasis, what your learners struggle with.
  4. Let it build one sample first (one chapter, one question, one screen). Give feedback on the sample, then let it do the rest in the same style.
  5. Ask it to check its own work before you see it: "Render the PDF as double-sided spreads and check page breaks", "Open the app at phone width and test every button", "Cross-check every memo mark against the paper."
  6. Moderate, then finalise. Files are labelled "For moderation" until you say they're final. Then: "Refresh my backup."

Starter prompts to copy:

Textbook: Use remote control on my computer. Read my hub and design lens. Plan a Grade [x] [subject] coursebook and matching worked-answer book from the sources in [folder]. Every curriculum statement must be covered. Show me the module list and one sample module before building the rest.
Exam paper: Use remote control on my computer. Read my hub. Build the Grade [x] [subject] [paper] with a matching memo overlay, from [sources]. Answer spaces sized from the worked answer. Show me the plan first. Save everything "For moderation".
Learning app: Use remote control on my computer. Read my hub and design lens. Build a phone-friendly practice app for [topic] with fresh numbers each time, hints only after an attempt, and a "check it on your own" mode. Host it on my GitHub Pages. No learner names or sign-in.
Under the hood · for IT staffMy books are generated, not typeset: one Python source file per module emits HTML, headless Chromium (Playwright) prints the PDF, and a build pass measures each answer space in the answer book so the coursebook matches it page for page. A width check flags any element wider than the 180 mm text block (one over-wide element makes Chrome scale every page). Apps are single-file HTML/JS (no build step) or Google Apps Script web apps when sign-in and a class spreadsheet are needed; Apps Script keeps learner data inside the school's Google Workspace, which is the right home for anything identifiable.

Keeping your work yours

Your textbooks, design lens and years of teaching knowledge are yours. Make sure your files agree.

  • Personal account, private backup. Keep a private copy in an account tied to your personal email (mine is a private GitHub repository). If you change schools, it comes with you.
  • Learner pages somewhere you own. Free GitHub Pages gives you a permanent link like yourname.github.io, not one that dies when you stop paying for an AI service.
  • Google Docs need their own backup. On your computer they are only shortcuts. Google Takeout can email you a full copy every two months; download it to a personal drive.
  • Learner privacy. No learner names, ID numbers, emails or marks in AI chats or public pages. Use learner numbers for mark analysis. Anything that records learner data runs inside the school's own system, with permission.
  • Check your contract about who owns materials you create, especially if you share them with other schools.
Under the hood · for IT staffA user GitHub Pages site needs a public repo named exactly <username>.github.io; keep source you don't want public in a separate private repo. GitHub accounts belong to the person, but if the only verified email is a school address, password resets and SSO sign-in die with the mailbox, so add a personal email or create the account on one. Takeout scheduled exports (every 2 months for a year) are free; zips over 2 GB use zip64. POPIA gives children's personal information extra protection (processing generally needs a competent person's consent or another lawful ground), so prefer pseudonymised IDs, keep identifiable data in Workspace, and never paste it into a consumer AI product.
