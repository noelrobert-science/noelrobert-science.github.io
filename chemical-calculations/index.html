Mass, molar mass and moles

Molar mass connects a sample's mass with its amount of substance.

Retrieve the quantities

Three symbols will guide the calculation. Have paper and a calculator ready. Write the quantity and unit for small m, capital M and n. Pause now. A question mark is useful if you are unsure; check it when you resume.

Check the quantities

Small m is the sample's mass in grams. Capital M is molar mass, in grams per mole: the mass of one mole. n is the amount of substance in moles. Correct your own labels before moving on.

Predict before calculating

Ten grams of magnesium oxide is our sample. One mole has a mass of forty grams. Should the sample contain less than one mole or more than one mole? Pause and write your prediction with a reason before doing arithmetic.

One quarter of the reference mass

The sample is one quarter of the mass of one mole. Its amount is therefore one quarter of a mole. These bars compare masses of the same substance. Keep that prediction in mind as we choose and use the equation.

Find the molar mass

Magnesium oxide has one magnesium for every oxygen in its formula. Using the provided atomic masses, twenty-four plus sixteen gives forty grams per mole. Count the formula subscripts before adding.

Choose, substitute, check

To find the amount, divide sample mass by molar mass. Ten divided by forty gives zero point two five moles. It agrees with our prediction. Keep units with given quantities and the final answer; substitute numbers in the working line.

Your next step

The starting equation is supplied. Complete the substitution and answer for eighteen grams. Pause and write now. If you need help, identify which number is sample mass and which is the mass of one mole. Try the step before continuing to the solution.

Check the supported attempt

Eighteen divided by forty gives zero point four five moles. If you reversed the fraction or multiplied, return to the question: how much of one mole's forty-gram mass do we have? Correct the line yourself. A hint helps you learn; the next fresh attempt needs less support.

Change the unknown

Now the unknown is mass. Starting from n equals m divided by M, multiply both sides by M. That gives mass equals amount multiplied by molar mass. Choose the equation from the quantity requested, rather than automatically dividing.

Try the reverse calculation

Zero point three zero moles is the amount. First predict the mass, compared with forty grams, then write the calculation. Pause now. Explain why multiplication makes sense this time.

Check the mass

Amount multiplied by molar mass gives twelve grams. Three tenths of a mole has three tenths of forty grams. The result is below forty grams, as predicted. Round final numerical answers to two decimal places unless told otherwise; keep full calculator values between steps.

A fresh independent attempt

Carbon dioxide changes the formula. Close the previous working and solve this on paper. Count the atoms represented in the formula, then find molar mass and choose your equation. Pause before continuing. This attempt should show what you can do without a hint.

Diagnose your answer

The two oxygens make the molar mass forty-four grams per mole. Twenty-two grams is half that mass, so the amount is zero point five zero moles. If you used twenty-eight for molar mass, you missed the oxygen subscript. Correct the formula count first.

Close the model and explain your equation choice from memory. Correct one error, then try fresh studio practice. When your teacher assigns a Canvas checkpoint, submit it independently for marking and review. Return after a gap and check whether you can still choose and use the method.

Why are atoms and molecules different?

A molecule is a group of bonded atoms. One H₂O molecule contains two H atoms and one O atom. So 1 mol of H₂O molecules contains 2 mol of H atoms and 1 mol of O atoms.

NaCl and Ca(OH)₂ are ionic compounds. We count formula units, not discrete molecules. Their formula gives the simplest ratio of ions.

Try it, then explain it

Set the mass to 44 g. Compare water and carbon dioxide. The masses match; do the numbers of molecules match? Use their molar masses to explain why.

Explore amount and volume

Move one slider at a time. More dissolved solute adds more ions. Increasing the final solution volume spreads the same ions out. Predict the concentration before revealing it.

Solute concentration, c
Final volume in dm³

Use the final volume of solution, not just the volume of water added.
1 dm³ = 1 000 cm³ = 1 litre.

Each symbol represents 0.02 mol of ions, not one ion. Circles and squares distinguish ion types; the colours are illustrative. This model assumes complete dissolution and dissociation; final volume is controlled independently.

Adding water

For dilution, the moles of solute stay the same. A larger final volume gives a smaller concentration.

c₁V₁ = c₂V₂

This applies when no solute is added, removed or reacted.

Mixing solutions

When solutions do not react, calculate the moles of the ion from each solution, add those moles, then divide by the combined volume.

c(ion) = total n(ion)total V

Assume volumes are additive when the question says so. If a reaction occurs, account for it first.

Connect this to percentage yield

The table shows the ideal reaction and its theoretical yield. Actual collected product may be lower because of losses, incomplete reaction or side reactions; the slider does not recalculate those processes.

Coefficients change amounts. Subscripts change substances.

In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, the ratio is 2 mol : 1 mol : 2 mol. It is not a mass ratio. Never change a formula’s subscripts to balance an equation.

This is a calculation model, not an experimental procedure.

Method checklist
  1. Balance the equation if a reaction is involved.
  2. Convert to moles using the correct units.
  3. Use the coefficient ratio between substances.
  4. Convert to the quantity requested.
  5. Keep calculator precision until your final answer; include its unit.

Constants

Use this course value at STP.

Solutions and reactions

Concentration

c = nV

Use final solution volume in dm³.

Balanced-equation ratios

n(A)a = n(B)b

For aA → bB. The limiting reagent has the smallest amount in moles divided by its coefficient.
